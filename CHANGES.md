Reviewer BF:
General comments
=============
This paper presents a timely and technically ambitious proof-of-concept study evaluating whether peripheral blood single-cell transcriptomic and T-cell receptor features can be used to predict chemo-immunotherapy response in early-stage hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. The topic is clinically relevant, as predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitor benefit in HR+ breast cancer remain an important unmet need. The use of multimodal single-cell data, TCR encodings, interpretable machine learning, and leave-one-patient-out cross-validation are notable strengths.
However, the manuscript has several major methodological and interpretive limitations that need to be addressed before the findings can be considered reliable or clinically meaningful. Most importantly, the analysis is based on only four patients, while model performance is reported primarily at the cell level across more than 100,000 cells. Because the response label is assigned at the patient level, individual cells are not independent observations. This creates a substantial risk that the models are learning patient-specific, timepoint-specific, batch-related, or cell-composition differences rather than generalizable predictors of treatment response. The very high reported AUROC values should therefore be interpreted with extreme caution.
In addition, the use of baseline, post-treatment, and recurrence samples raises an important question about whether the model is truly predicting treatment response or instead identifying immune states associated with treatment exposure, recurrence, or known response status. The manuscript would be strengthened by clearer separation of baseline prediction, on-treatment monitoring, and post-treatment/recurrence characterization. Overall, the study is best framed as an exploratory hypothesis-generating analysis rather than a validated predictive model.
Specific comments
=============
Major comments
---------------------
1. The title overstates the level of validation. The title refers to a “Model Development and Validation Study,” but the analysis appears to use one very small public cohort with internal leave-one-patient-out cross-validation only. There is no independent external validation cohort. Please consider revising the title to reflect an exploratory or proof-of-concept model development study with internal validation.
2. The cohort size is extremely small for predictive modeling. The study includes only four patients, with two responders and two non-responders. This is a major limitation for machine learning model development, especially for deep learning models. The manuscript should more prominently acknowledge that the effective sample size for response prediction is four patients, not 100,067 cells.
3. Cell-level performance metrics may be misleading. The response label is defined at the patient level, but performance is reported at the cell level. Since thousands of cells from the same patient share the same outcome label, the cells are not statistically independent. High cell-level AUROC and accuracy may reflect within-patient similarity rather than true response prediction. Please report patient-level or sample-level performance by aggregating cell-level predictions within each held-out patient/sample.
4. Leave-one-patient-out cross-validation has only four outer folds. LOPO cross-validation is appropriate in concept, but with only four patients the model evaluation is highly unstable. Please report fold-level performance for each held-out patient rather than only pooled cell-level metrics. Confidence intervals should reflect patient-level uncertainty, not cell-level bootstrapping alone.
5. Bootstrapping should account for clustering by patient. The manuscript states that uncertainty was quantified through nonparametric bootstrapping. If bootstrapping was performed at the cell level, it likely underestimates uncertainty because cells are nested within patients. Please use patient-level or sample-level bootstrap/resampling, or clearly state the limitations of cell-level uncertainty estimates.
6. Clarify whether the model is predicting response or detecting treatment-associated immune states. The dataset includes baseline, post-treatment, and recurrence samples. If post-treatment and recurrence samples are included in model training and evaluation, the model may not be predicting response before treatment. It may instead be classifying immune states that emerge after therapy or recurrence. A baseline-only analysis is essential if the claim is treatment response prediction.
7. Recurrence samples may introduce outcome leakage. Including a recurrence specimen, especially from a non-responder, may make it easier for the model to distinguish disease course rather than prospective treatment response. Please justify inclusion of recurrence samples and perform sensitivity analyses excluding recurrence samples.
8. Timepoint distribution by patient and response group should be clearly shown. Please provide a table showing each patient, response status, timepoints available, number of cells per sample, number/proportion with productive TCRs, and sequencing modalities available. This is necessary to assess whether response labels are confounded by timepoint availability, recurrence status, or sequencing completeness.
9. Potential data leakage from feature engineering needs clarification. The manuscript states that StandardScaler was fit within training folds, which is good. However, it is unclear whether HVG selection, PCA, UMAP, k-mer vocabulary selection, imputation, and dimensionality reduction were also fit only on training data within each LOPO fold. If these steps were performed on the full dataset before cross-validation, this would introduce leakage.
10. Use of UMAP dimensions as model features is concerning. The gene-enhanced feature set appears to include UMAP dimensions. UMAP is primarily a visualization method and can be unstable, non-linear, and sensitive to global dataset structure. If UMAP was computed on all cells before cross-validation, it may also introduce data leakage. Please justify its use as a predictive feature or remove it from supervised modeling.
11. TCR feature missingness may be confounded with patient or sample identity. Approximately 21% of cells lacked productive beta-chain TCR information, and one sample lacked paired TCR sequencing entirely. Missingness may not be random and could reflect sample quality, sequencing modality, patient identity, or timepoint. Please analyze whether TCR missingness differs by patient, response group, or timepoint.
12. Sample-level repertoire features may leak patient identity. Features such as Shannon entropy and clonality are computed at the sample or repertoire level. If assigned to individual cells, these features may encode sample or patient identity rather than cell-intrinsic biology. Please clarify how these features were used and whether models remain predictive without sample-level repertoire summaries.
13. Nested hyperparameter tuning is underpowered. With only four patients, the outer LOPO fold leaves only three patients for training. Inner cross-validation using patient groups is therefore extremely limited. The manuscript should acknowledge that hyperparameter tuning is unstable and cannot support strong claims about optimal model architecture.
14. Deep learning models are not justified by the dataset size. The manuscript evaluates MLP, CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer models. Given the effective sample size of four patients, these architectures are highly prone to overfitting, even if many cells are available. Please either substantially temper claims about deep learning superiority or provide stronger regularization, permutation testing, and external validation.
15. Permutation or negative-control analyses are needed. The manuscript states that permutation-based null tests were not implemented. Given the small cohort and high reported AUCs, label permutation, patient-label shuffling, timepoint-shuffling, or batch-control analyses would be very useful to determine whether performance exceeds what could be achieved by learning patient identity or technical structure.
16. The reported AUC of 1.0 for one model requires explanation. Table 7 reports a tcr_enhanced MLP with AUC of 1.0 but relatively poor accuracy and specificity. This unusual combination should be explained. It may reflect thresholding issues, class imbalance, calibration problems, or unstable estimates.
17. The manuscript should separate biological discovery from clinical prediction. The analysis may provide useful biological hypotheses regarding cytotoxicity, interferon response, and TCR-associated immune states. However, this is different from developing a clinically deployable predictive model. Please frame the findings as exploratory biological signals unless validated in larger independent cohorts.
18. Claims about TCR encodings improving calibration need support. The Results state that TCR encodings improved calibration, but calibration metrics such as Brier score, calibration curves, expected calibration error, or decision-curve analysis are not clearly reported. Please provide quantitative calibration results or soften this claim.
19. The manuscript should include patient-level confusion matrices or prediction summaries. Since the clinically relevant unit is the patient, please show how each held-out patient was classified after aggregating cell-level predictions. This would make the model’s actual predictive performance more transparent.
20. Clarify the endpoint and timing of response assessment. The endpoint is defined as residual cancer burden at surgery, with responders as RCB 0/I and non-responders as RCB II/III. Please clarify whether all samples used for prediction were collected before this endpoint was known. If not, the analysis should not be described as prospective prediction.
21. The Methods section should clearly state this is a secondary analysis of public data. The subsection titled “Recruitment” may imply that the authors recruited participants. Since the dataset was published by Sun et al. and downloaded from GSE300475, this should be described as a retrospective secondary analysis of publicly available data.
22. The analysis repository should be directly accessible. The manuscript states that code and processed feature matrices are archived on GitHub and available upon request. If the work is intended to be reproducible, please provide the GitHub URL, package versions, random seeds, and processed feature matrices or scripts to regenerate them.
23. The limitations section should be moved earlier or strengthened in the Abstract/Conclusion. The limitations are important but appear after extensive claims of strong model performance. The Abstract and Conclusion should more clearly state that the findings are exploratory and not ready for clinical use.
24. The manuscript should avoid implying clinical deployment from current results. Statements about clinical translation, treatment de-escalation/intensification, and routine clinical use are premature based on four patients. These ideas can be retained as future directions, but they should not be presented as direct implications of the current model.
25. Reference quality and formatting require careful review. Several references appear incorrectly formatted or potentially mismatched to the cited claim. For example, the Deep Learning reference appears dated as 2003, the “Attention is all you need” reference appears as 2025, and the final reference includes unrelated hypertension/stroke text. The reference list needs a thorough audit.

Paper shows impressive metrics must be framed with extreme caution. Author should emphasize that while the methodology is technically sophisticated, the small sample size (N=4) means the reported performance metrics are highly susceptible to overfitting and may reflect within-patient consistency rather than true population generalizability. This repositioning maintains confidence in the data while respecting the current limitations of sample size.

Reviewer BF:
General comments
=============
This paper presents a timely and technically ambitious proof-of-concept study evaluating whether peripheral blood single-cell transcriptomic and T-cell receptor features can be used to predict chemo-immunotherapy response in early-stage hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. The topic is clinically relevant, as predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitor benefit in HR+ breast cancer remain an important unmet need. The use of multimodal single-cell data, TCR encodings, interpretable machine learning, and leave-one-patient-out cross-validation are notable strengths.
However, the manuscript has several major methodological and interpretive limitations that need to be addressed before the findings can be considered reliable or clinically meaningful. Most importantly, the analysis is based on only four patients, while model performance is reported primarily at the cell level across more than 100,000 cells. Because the response label is assigned at the patient level, individual cells are not independent observations. This creates a substantial risk that the models are learning patient-specific, timepoint-specific, batch-related, or cell-composition differences rather than generalizable predictors of treatment response. The very high reported AUROC values should therefore be interpreted with extreme caution.
In addition, the use of baseline, post-treatment, and recurrence samples raises an important question about whether the model is truly predicting treatment response or instead identifying immune states associated with treatment exposure, recurrence, or known response status. The manuscript would be strengthened by clearer separation of baseline prediction, on-treatment monitoring, and post-treatment/recurrence characterization. Overall, the study is best framed as an exploratory hypothesis-generating analysis rather than a validated predictive model.
Specific comments
=============
Major comments
---------------------
1. The title overstates the level of validation. The title refers to a “Model Development and Validation Study,” but the analysis appears to use one very small public cohort with internal leave-one-patient-out cross-validation only. There is no independent external validation cohort. Please consider revising the title to reflect an exploratory or proof-of-concept model development study with internal validation.
2. The cohort size is extremely small for predictive modeling. The study includes only four patients, with two responders and two non-responders. This is a major limitation for machine learning model development, especially for deep learning models. The manuscript should more prominently acknowledge that the effective sample size for response prediction is four patients, not 100,067 cells.
3. Cell-level performance metrics may be misleading. The response label is defined at the patient level, but performance is reported at the cell level. Since thousands of cells from the same patient share the same outcome label, the cells are not statistically independent. High cell-level AUROC and accuracy may reflect within-patient similarity rather than true response prediction. Please report patient-level or sample-level performance by aggregating cell-level predictions within each held-out patient/sample.
4. Leave-one-patient-out cross-validation has only four outer folds. LOPO cross-validation is appropriate in concept, but with only four patients the model evaluation is highly unstable. Please report fold-level performance for each held-out patient rather than only pooled cell-level metrics. Confidence intervals should reflect patient-level uncertainty, not cell-level bootstrapping alone.
5. Bootstrapping should account for clustering by patient. The manuscript states that uncertainty was quantified through nonparametric bootstrapping. If bootstrapping was performed at the cell level, it likely underestimates uncertainty because cells are nested within patients. Please use patient-level or sample-level bootstrap/resampling, or clearly state the limitations of cell-level uncertainty estimates.
6. Clarify whether the model is predicting response or detecting treatment-associated immune states. The dataset includes baseline, post-treatment, and recurrence samples. If post-treatment and recurrence samples are included in model training and evaluation, the model may not be predicting response before treatment. It may instead be classifying immune states that emerge after therapy or recurrence. A baseline-only analysis is essential if the claim is treatment response prediction.
7. Recurrence samples may introduce outcome leakage. Including a recurrence specimen, especially from a non-responder, may make it easier for the model to distinguish disease course rather than prospective treatment response. Please justify inclusion of recurrence samples and perform sensitivity analyses excluding recurrence samples.
8. Timepoint distribution by patient and response group should be clearly shown. Please provide a table showing each patient, response status, timepoints available, number of cells per sample, number/proportion with productive TCRs, and sequencing modalities available. This is necessary to assess whether response labels are confounded by timepoint availability, recurrence status, or sequencing completeness.
9. Potential data leakage from feature engineering needs clarification. The manuscript states that StandardScaler was fit within training folds, which is good. However, it is unclear whether HVG selection, PCA, UMAP, k-mer vocabulary selection, imputation, and dimensionality reduction were also fit only on training data within each LOPO fold. If these steps were performed on the full dataset before cross-validation, this would introduce leakage.
10. Use of UMAP dimensions as model features is concerning. The gene-enhanced feature set appears to include UMAP dimensions. UMAP is primarily a visualization method and can be unstable, non-linear, and sensitive to global dataset structure. If UMAP was computed on all cells before cross-validation, it may also introduce data leakage. Please justify its use as a predictive feature or remove it from supervised modeling.
11. TCR feature missingness may be confounded with patient or sample identity. Approximately 21% of cells lacked productive beta-chain TCR information, and one sample lacked paired TCR sequencing entirely. Missingness may not be random and could reflect sample quality, sequencing modality, patient identity, or timepoint. Please analyze whether TCR missingness differs by patient, response group, or timepoint.
12. Sample-level repertoire features may leak patient identity. Features such as Shannon entropy and clonality are computed at the sample or repertoire level. If assigned to individual cells, these features may encode sample or patient identity rather than cell-intrinsic biology. Please clarify how these features were used and whether models remain predictive without sample-level repertoire summaries.
13. Nested hyperparameter tuning is underpowered. With only four patients, the outer LOPO fold leaves only three patients for training. Inner cross-validation using patient groups is therefore extremely limited. The manuscript should acknowledge that hyperparameter tuning is unstable and cannot support strong claims about optimal model architecture.
14. Deep learning models are not justified by the dataset size. The manuscript evaluates MLP, CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer models. Given the effective sample size of four patients, these architectures are highly prone to overfitting, even if many cells are available. Please either substantially temper claims about deep learning superiority or provide stronger regularization, permutation testing, and external validation.
15. Permutation or negative-control analyses are needed. The manuscript states that permutation-based null tests were not implemented. Given the small cohort and high reported AUCs, label permutation, patient-label shuffling, timepoint-shuffling, or batch-control analyses would be very useful to determine whether performance exceeds what could be achieved by learning patient identity or technical structure.
16. The reported AUC of 1.0 for one model requires explanation. Table 7 reports a tcr_enhanced MLP with AUC of 1.0 but relatively poor accuracy and specificity. This unusual combination should be explained. It may reflect thresholding issues, class imbalance, calibration problems, or unstable estimates.
17. The manuscript should separate biological discovery from clinical prediction. The analysis may provide useful biological hypotheses regarding cytotoxicity, interferon response, and TCR-associated immune states. However, this is different from developing a clinically deployable predictive model. Please frame the findings as exploratory biological signals unless validated in larger independent cohorts.
18. Claims about TCR encodings improving calibration need support. The Results state that TCR encodings improved calibration, but calibration metrics such as Brier score, calibration curves, expected calibration error, or decision-curve analysis are not clearly reported. Please provide quantitative calibration results or soften this claim.
19. The manuscript should include patient-level confusion matrices or prediction summaries. Since the clinically relevant unit is the patient, please show how each held-out patient was classified after aggregating cell-level predictions. This would make the model’s actual predictive performance more transparent.
20. Clarify the endpoint and timing of response assessment. The endpoint is defined as residual cancer burden at surgery, with responders as RCB 0/I and non-responders as RCB II/III. Please clarify whether all samples used for prediction were collected before this endpoint was known. If not, the analysis should not be described as prospective prediction.
21. The Methods section should clearly state this is a secondary analysis of public data. The subsection titled “Recruitment” may imply that the authors recruited participants. Since the dataset was published by Sun et al. and downloaded from GSE300475, this should be described as a retrospective secondary analysis of publicly available data.
22. The analysis repository should be directly accessible. The manuscript states that code and processed feature matrices are archived on GitHub and available upon request. If the work is intended to be reproducible, please provide the GitHub URL, package versions, random seeds, and processed feature matrices or scripts to regenerate them.
23. The limitations section should be moved earlier or strengthened in the Abstract/Conclusion. The limitations are important but appear after extensive claims of strong model performance. The Abstract and Conclusion should more clearly state that the findings are exploratory and not ready for clinical use.
24. The manuscript should avoid implying clinical deployment from current results. Statements about clinical translation, treatment de-escalation/intensification, and routine clinical use are premature based on four patients. These ideas can be retained as future directions, but they should not be presented as direct implications of the current model.
25. Reference quality and formatting require careful review. Several references appear incorrectly formatted or potentially mismatched to the cited claim. For example, the Deep Learning reference appears dated as 2003, the “Attention is all you need” reference appears as 2025, and the final reference includes unrelated hypertension/stroke text. The reference list needs a thorough audit.
